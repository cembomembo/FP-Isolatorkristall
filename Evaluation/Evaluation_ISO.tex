\section{Introduction}

Ruby is a red variety of corundum (Al$_2$O$_3$) whose color originates from small amounts of Cr$^{3+}$ ions replacing Al$^{3+}$ sites in the crystal.
Unlike pure corundum, which is colorless and transparent, the chromium impurities introduce electronic states that interact with visible light and give rise to characteristic absorption features.
In this experiment we measure the wavelength-dependent transmission of a ruby crystal using a two-beam absorption spectrometer.
From the recorded absorption bands we aim to understand the optical origin of the ruby color and relate the observed transitions to the crystal-field splitting of the Cr$^{3+}$ ions.
The experiment therefore provides a direct link between optical spectroscopy and the basic concepts of crystal-field theory as applied to doped insulating crystals.



% --- Physical Principles ---
\section{Physical Principles}
\subsection{Properties of Pure Corundum}

Corundum is the host crystal in which the optical properties of ruby originate.
Before discussing chromium impurities it is useful to understand both the geometric arrangement of ions and the electronic structure of the pure material.
These two aspects together explain why undoped Al$_2$O$_3$ is transparent and colorless, and they form the basis for understanding how Cr$^{3+}$ impurities modify its optical properties.

\subsubsection{Crystal Structure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:corundum}, corundum consists of Al$^{3+}$ cations and O$^{2-}$ anions arranged in a slightly distorted octahedral coordination \cite{ISOAnleitung}.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{../resources/figures/CorundumStructure.jpg}
    \caption{The distorted-octahedral oxygen-ligand environment around an Al$^{3+}$ ion in corundum Al$_2$O$_3$ is shown in two ways, (A) and (B), and compared with a regular octahedron (C) \cite{webexhibits_causes_of_color}.}
    \label{fig:corundum}
\end{figure}

Each aluminum ion is surrounded by six oxygen ions, and these oxygen ligands generate a static electrostatic field $O_h$ at every cation site.
Because Al$^{3+}$ has the electron configuration [Ne], its outer shells are completely filled and it has no $d$-electrons available for optical excitations in the visible range.
As a result the Al$^{3+}$ sites act essentially as stable point charges that contribute to the mechanical stability of the lattice without interacting with visible light.

\subsubsection{Electronic Structure and Band Gap}

The transparency of pure corundum is a consequence of its electronic band structure.
In a crystalline solid the atomic orbitals combine to form energy bands, and the electrons occupy the lowest available bands up to the valence band.
Above the valence band lies the conduction band, and the energy separation between the two is called the band gap.
This band structure is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bandtheory}.

In Al$_2$O$_3$ this band gap is very large, typically several electronvolts.
The Fermi level lies well inside this gap, which means that the valence band is completely filled and the conduction band is completely empty.
Electrons in the valence band therefore cannot be thermally excited into the conduction band, and the crystal contains no mobile charge carriers.
This is why corundum behaves as an electrical insulator.

Because the band gap is much larger than the photon energies of visible light, no direct electronic transitions between valence band and conduction band can be induced in the visible range.
Pure corundum therefore does not absorb visible wavelengths and appears colorless.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../resources/figures/BandTheory.png}
    \caption{Comparison of the electronic band structures of a conductor (metal), a semiconductor, and an insulator.
    The large band gap in the insulator case explains why pure Al$_2$O$_3$ does not absorb visible light \cite{wikipedia_bandtheory}.}
    \label{fig:bandtheory}
\end{figure}

These structural and electronic properties describe the behaviour of undoped corundum.
In the next subsection we introduce Cr$^{3+}$ ions as substitutional point defects.
Their partially filled $3d$-shell leads to additional electronic states inside the wide band gap of Al$_2$O$_3$, and these states give rise to the characteristic absorption features observed in ruby.


\subsection{Crystal-Field Splitting of the Cr\texorpdfstring{$^{3+}$}{} d-States}

When a Cr$^{3+}$ ion replaces an Al$^{3+}$ ion in corundum it sits in the same slightly distorted octahedral site formed by six surrounding O$^{2-}$ ions.
In contrast to Al$^{3+}$, which has the closed-shell configuration [Ne], a Cr$^{3+}$ ion has the configuration [Ar]\,3$d^{3}$ and therefore brings three $d$-electrons into this ligand environment.
These three electrons feel the electrostatic repulsion of the nearby oxygen ions, and this changes the energy of the $d$-orbitals compared to the free ion.

\subsubsection{Free Ion Picture}

Before looking at the crystal field it is helpful to recall how the electrons behave in the free Cr$^{3+}$ ion.
There are five different $d$-orbitals, and each of them can hold two electrons according to the Pauli principle.
In the free ion all five orbitals have the same energy, so the three electrons do not prefer any particular one.
They distribute themselves according to Hund’s rules, which tell us that the electrons first occupy different orbitals with parallel spins.
This arrangement gives a total spin of $S = \tfrac{3}{2}$ and a total orbital angular momentum of $L = 3$, and this combination is written as the free-ion term ${}^{4}F$.

The superscript $4$ in ${}^{4}F$ means that the state has a spin multiplicity of $2S+1 = 4$.
The letter $F$ is simply the spectroscopic label for $L = 3$.
In principle this ${}^{4}F$ state is split into several closely spaced levels called $J$-levels because spin and orbital motion can couple.
For Cr$^{3+}$ these splittings are very small, because spin–orbit coupling is weak, so we do not consider the individual $J$-levels in this experiment~\cite{ISOAnleitung}.

\subsubsection{Splitting of the \texorpdfstring{$^{4}F$}{} Term in an Octahedral Field}

When the ion is placed in the octahedral environment of corundum the spherical symmetry of the free ion is lost.
This is because the five $3d$-orbitals are affected differently depending on their orientation relative to the oxygen ligands, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:d_orbitals}.
Orbitals that point between the ligands ($d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$, $d_{yz}$) experience less repulsion and therefore lie at lower energy, while those that point directly toward the ligands ($d_{z^{2}}$ and $d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}$) experience stronger repulsion and are pushed to higher energy.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../resources/figures/dOrbitals.png}
    \caption{Shapes of the five $d$-orbitals relative to the coordinate axes and the ligand directions in an octahedral environment.
    Orbitals that point along the axes experience stronger repulsion than those pointing between the axes \cite{libretexts_d_orbitals}.}
    \label{fig:d_orbitals}
\end{figure}

If we imagine a single electron, the five $d$-orbitals would simply split into a lower-energy group of three and a higher-energy group of two.
These groups are often labelled $t_{2g}$ (threefold) and $e_{g}$ (twofold).
In that simple picture there would be only two different energy levels.

However, Cr$^{3+}$ has three $d$-electrons, and they interact with each other.
The crystal field therefore acts on the combined many-electron state, not on each orbital separately.
Because of this, the free-ion term ${}^{4}F$ does not split into just two levels but into three states with different energies.

These three states are called ${}^{4}A_{2}$, ${}^{4}T_{2}$, and ${}^{4}T_{1}$.
The letter $A$ means that the state is non-degenerate (only one independent wavefunction), while the letter $T$ means that the state is triply degenerate (three wavefunctions with the same energy).
The subscripts $1$ and $2$ distinguish two different types of symmetry within the same degeneracy.
The state ${}^{4}A_{2}$ is the lowest in energy and is the ground state of Cr$^{3+}$ in corundum.
The states ${}^{4}T_{2}$ and ${}^{4}T_{1}$ lie higher in energy and correspond to two different excited configurations of the three-electron system.


\subsection{Optical Transitions and Absorption Spectrum}

\subsubsection{Overview of the Energy Levels}

The splitting of the Cr$^{3+}$ $d$-orbitals creates a set of energy levels that an electron can move between.
An overview of these states is given by the Tanabe-Sugano diagram for a $d^{3}$ ion (Fig.~\ref{fig:tanabe_sugano}).
The solid lines represent the quartet states, which originate from the free-ion ground term ${}^{4}F$, while the dashed lines represent doublet states that arise from higher-lying free-ion terms such as ${}^{2}G$.
Quartet states have total spin $S=\tfrac{3}{2}$ (superscript $4$), while doublet states have total spin $S=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (superscript $2$).
Transitions between states of the same spin are strong, whereas transitions between quartet and doublet states are much weaker.

\subsubsection{Broad Absorption Bands}

When a photon has exactly the right energy, one of the $d$-electrons can be excited from the ground state into one of the two excited quartet states.
The light at exactly this wavelength is removed from the transmitted beam, so the crystal absorbs certain parts of the spectrum more strongly than others.
Because the ${}^{4}T_{2}$ and ${}^{4}T_{1}$ states lie at different energies, the absorption spectrum shows two separate features rather than only one.

In a real crystal the surrounding ions are not perfectly fixed but vibrate, and the distances between the Cr$^{3+}$ ion and the ligands are slightly distorted.
These effects mean that the exact energy required for the excitation is not the same in every chromium site.
As a result the absorption does not occur at one sharp wavelength, but is spread out over a broader range, producing the wide absorption bands characteristic of ruby.

For Cr$^{3+}$ in corundum two such broad bands appear in the visible region.
The so-called Y band (${}^{4}A_{2} \rightarrow {}^{4}T_{2}$) and U band (${}^{4}A_{2} \rightarrow {}^{4}T_{1}$).
Their positions determine which parts of the spectrum are absorbed most strongly.
Since these two bands absorb most strongly in the green and blue part of the spectrum, red light is transmitted.
This is why ruby appears red in both transmission and reflected light.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{../resources/figures/TanabeSugano.png}
    \caption{Tanabe-Sugano diagram for a $d^{3}$ ion showing the dependence of the electronic term energies on the crystal-field strength $Dq/B$.
    At $Dq/B = 0$ the free-ion ground term ${}^{4}F$ appears as a single level, which splits into three states: the orange ${}^{4}A_{2}$ ground state, the blue ${}^{4}T_{2}$ state, and the green ${}^{4}T_{1}$ state \cite{wikipedia_tanabe_sugano_d3}.}
    \label{fig:tanabe_sugano}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Forbidden Transitions}

In the deep red region the spectrum of ruby shows a few very weak and narrow features in addition to the broad U and Y bands.
These originate from transitions into doublet states such as ${}^{2}T_{1}$, ${}^{2}T_{2}$ and ${}^{2}E$.
Transitions that change the total spin are often called ``forbidden'', because they have a much lower probability than normal spin-conserving transitions.
However, real crystals are never perfectly symmetric, so small distortions and lattice vibrations mix the electronic states slightly and these transitions become weakly allowed.
As a result they appear as tiny absorption features in the red part of the spectrum.

The doublet state ${}^{2}E$ also plays an important role because electrons that are first excited into ${}^{4}T_{2}$ can relax non-radiatively into this level.
From there they can return to the ground state ${}^{4}A_{2}$ by emitting red light.
This radiative decay produces two very sharp emission lines around 694\,nm, often referred to as the R-lines.
They do not show up as absorption dips in this experiment, but they belong to the same group of spin-forbidden transitions and one of them forms the basis of the well-known ruby laser~\cite{kitzmann_molecular_ruby_2022,wikipedia_ruby_pressure_scale}.


\subsection{Determining Splitting Energy from Tanabe-Sugano Diagram}

The Tanabe–Sugano diagram for a $d^{3}$ ion, seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:tanabe_sugano}, shows how the energies of the electronic states split and shift as a function of the crystal-field strength.
In this diagram the term energies are expressed in spectroscopic units of $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, and the Racah parameter $B$ has the same units, so the vertical axis plots the dimensionless ratio $E/B$.
The crystal-field strength $Dq$ is likewise an energy expressed in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, which is why the horizontal axis shows the dimensionless ratio $Dq/B$.
This diagram allows the energies of the optical transitions to be related directly to $Dq$, which quantifies how strongly the octahedral ligand field separates the lower and higher groups of $d$-orbitals.

In ruby the ground state is ${}^{4}A_{2}$, and the two broad absorption bands observed in the visible region correspond to excitations into the ${}^{4}T_{2}$ and ${}^{4}T_{1}$ states.
These are the Y band (${}^{4}A_{2} \rightarrow {}^{4}T_{2}$) and the U band (${}^{4}A_{2} \rightarrow {}^{4}T_{1}$), and their energies reflect the size of the crystal-field splitting.

To compare measured wavelengths with the diagram, the photon energies of the transitions are obtained from

\[
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda},
\]

or in spectroscopic units from the wavenumber definition

\[
\tilde{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda}.
\]

By measuring the central wavelengths of the U and Y absorption bands and converting them into transition energies or wavenumbers, the corresponding positions can be located on the Tanabe–Sugano diagram.
Using the given Racah parameter $B = 765\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, the ratio $Dq/B$ can be read off from the intersections, and from this the crystal-field splitting energy
\[
\Delta = 10Dq
\]
of the Cr$^{3+}$ ion in corundum can be determined.
This procedure provides a direct connection between the measured absorption spectrum and the underlying electronic structure of the chromium impurity.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Two-Beam Absorption Spectrometer}
In order to record the optical absorption spectrum of a ruby crystal a two-beam absorption spectrometer is employed (see Figure~\ref{fig:spectrometer}).
Light source used correspond to a Xenon high-pressure lamp. Its beam passes an iris diaphragm and then is divided is divided 
into two parallel beams using a beam splitter. One beam interacts with the ruby sample, while the second beam propagates
through an aperture as a reference. The beams are then again recombined using a set of lenses and mirrors.

The recombined beam then interacts with a monochromator, which selects a single wavelength at a time. The monochromator (symbol \textit{M} on the figure) is driven by an electric motor that scans the wavelength continuously across the region of $350-720$nm.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../resources/figures/spectrometer.png}
    \caption{Sketch of the spectrometer setup.}
    \label{fig:spectrometer}
\end{figure}

A mechanical chopper (symbol \textit{C} on the figure) placed in the two-beam section of the setup periodically and alternatively blocks both beams, so that the intensity at the photomultiplier (symbol \textit{PM} on the figure) alternates between the probe and reference signals at the chopping frequency. 
The chopper controller provides a reference signal for the lock-in amplifier. To suppress noise and lamp intensity fluctuations, the lock-in amplifier weights the probe contribution with positive sign and the reference contribution with negative sign. Its low-pass filtered output is therefore proportional to the intensity difference $I_{\mathrm{Probe}} - I_{\mathrm{Referenz}}$.
Finally, the analog output of the lock-in amplifier is digitized by an A/D converter and recorded by the computer as a function of the scanned monochromator wavelength.
