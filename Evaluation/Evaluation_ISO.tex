\section{Introduction}

Ruby is a red variety of corundum (Al$_2$O$_3$) whose color originates from small amounts of Cr$^{3+}$ ions replacing Al$^{3+}$ sites in the crystal.
Unlike pure corundum, which is colorless and transparent, the chromium impurities introduce electronic states that interact with visible light and give rise to characteristic absorption features.
In this experiment we measure the wavelength-dependent transmission of a ruby crystal using a two-beam absorption spectrometer.
From the recorded absorption bands we aim to understand the optical origin of the ruby color and relate the observed transitions to the crystal-field splitting of the Cr$^{3+}$ ions.
The experiment therefore provides a direct link between optical spectroscopy and the basic concepts of crystal-field theory as applied to doped insulating crystals.



% --- Physical Principles ---
\section{Physical Principles}
\subsection{Properties of Pure Corundum}

Corundum is the host crystal in which the optical properties of ruby originate.
Before discussing chromium impurities it is useful to understand both the geometric arrangement of ions and the electronic structure of the pure material.
These two aspects together explain why undoped Al$_2$O$_3$ is transparent and colorless, and they form the basis for understanding how Cr$^{3+}$ impurities modify its optical properties.

\subsubsection{Crystal Structure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:corundum}, corundum consists of Al$^{3+}$ cations and O$^{2-}$ anions arranged in a slightly distorted octahedral coordination~\cite{ISOAnleitung}.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{../resources/figures/CorundumStructure.jpg}
    \caption{The distorted-octahedral oxygen-ligand environment around an Al$^{3+}$ ion in corundum Al$_2$O$_3$ is shown in two ways, (A) and (B), and compared with a regular octahedron (C)~\cite{webexhibits_causes_of_color}.}
    \label{fig:corundum}
\end{figure}

Each aluminum ion is surrounded by six oxygen ions, and these oxygen ligands generate a static electrostatic field $O_h$ at every cation site.
Because Al$^{3+}$ has the electron configuration [Ne], its outer shells are completely filled and it has no $d$-electrons available for optical excitations in the visible range.
As a result the Al$^{3+}$ sites act essentially as stable point charges that contribute to the mechanical stability of the lattice without interacting with visible light.

\subsubsection{Electronic Structure and Band Gap}

The transparency of pure corundum is a consequence of its electronic band structure.
In a crystalline solid the atomic orbitals combine to form energy bands, and the electrons occupy the lowest available bands up to the valence band.
Above the valence band lies the conduction band, and the energy separation between the two is called the band gap.
This band structure is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bandtheory}.

In Al$_2$O$_3$ this band gap is very large, typically several electronvolts.
The Fermi level lies well inside this gap, which means that the valence band is completely filled and the conduction band is completely empty.
Electrons in the valence band therefore cannot be thermally excited into the conduction band, and the crystal contains no mobile charge carriers.
This is why corundum behaves as an electrical insulator.

Because the band gap is much larger than the photon energies of visible light, no direct electronic transitions between valence band and conduction band can be induced in the visible range.
Pure corundum therefore does not absorb visible wavelengths and appears colorless.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../resources/figures/BandTheory.png}
    \caption{Comparison of the electronic band structures of a conductor (metal), a semiconductor, and an insulator.
    The large band gap in the insulator case explains why pure Al$_2$O$_3$ does not absorb visible light~\cite{wikipedia_bandtheory}.}
    \label{fig:bandtheory}
\end{figure}

These structural and electronic properties describe the behaviour of undoped corundum.
In the next subsection we introduce Cr$^{3+}$ ions as substitutional point defects.
Their partially filled $3d$-shell leads to additional electronic states inside the wide band gap of Al$_2$O$_3$, and these states give rise to the characteristic absorption features observed in ruby.


\subsection{Crystal-Field Splitting of the Cr\texorpdfstring{$^{3+}$}{} d-States}

When a Cr$^{3+}$ ion replaces an Al$^{3+}$ ion in corundum it sits in the same slightly distorted octahedral site formed by six surrounding O$^{2-}$ ions.
In contrast to Al$^{3+}$, which has the closed-shell configuration [Ne], a Cr$^{3+}$ ion has the configuration [Ar]\,3$d^{3}$ and therefore brings three $d$-electrons into this ligand environment.
These three electrons feel the electrostatic repulsion of the nearby oxygen ions, and this changes the energy of the $d$-orbitals compared to the free ion.

\subsubsection{Free Ion Picture}

Before looking at the crystal field it is helpful to recall how the electrons behave in the free Cr$^{3+}$ ion.
There are five different $d$-orbitals, and each of them can hold two electrons according to the Pauli principle.
In the free ion all five orbitals have the same energy, so the three electrons do not prefer any particular one.
They distribute themselves according to Hund’s rules, which tell us that the electrons first occupy different orbitals with parallel spins.
This arrangement gives a total spin of $S = \tfrac{3}{2}$ and a total orbital angular momentum of $L = 3$, and this combination is written as the free-ion term ${}^{4}F$.

The superscript $4$ in ${}^{4}F$ means that the state has a spin multiplicity of $2S+1 = 4$.
The letter $F$ is simply the spectroscopic label for $L = 3$.
In principle this ${}^{4}F$ state is split into several closely spaced levels called $J$-levels because spin and orbital motion can couple.
For Cr$^{3+}$ these splittings are very small, because spin–orbit coupling is weak, so we do not consider the individual $J$-levels in this experiment~\cite{ISOAnleitung}.

\subsubsection{Splitting of the \texorpdfstring{$^{4}F$}{} Term in an Octahedral Field}

When the ion is placed in the octahedral environment of corundum the spherical symmetry of the free ion is lost.
This is because the five $3d$-orbitals are affected differently depending on their orientation relative to the oxygen ligands, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:d_orbitals}.
Orbitals that point between the ligands ($d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$, $d_{yz}$) experience less repulsion and therefore lie at lower energy, while those that point directly toward the ligands ($d_{z^{2}}$ and $d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}$) experience stronger repulsion and are pushed to higher energy.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../resources/figures/dOrbitals.png}
    \caption{Shapes of the five $d$-orbitals relative to the coordinate axes and the ligand directions in an octahedral environment.
    Orbitals that point along the axes experience stronger repulsion than those pointing between the axes~\cite{libretexts_d_orbitals}.}
    \label{fig:d_orbitals}
\end{figure}

If we imagine a single electron, the five $d$-orbitals would simply split into a lower-energy group of three and a higher-energy group of two.
These groups are often labelled $t_{2g}$ (threefold) and $e_{g}$ (twofold).
In that simple picture there would be only two different energy levels.

However, Cr$^{3+}$ has three $d$-electrons, and they interact with each other.
The crystal field therefore acts on the combined many-electron state, not on each orbital separately.
Because of this, the free-ion term ${}^{4}F$ does not split into just two levels but into three states with different energies.

These three states are called ${}^{4}A_{2}$, ${}^{4}T_{2}$, and ${}^{4}T_{1}$.
The letter $A$ means that the state is non-degenerate (only one independent wavefunction), while the letter $T$ means that the state is triply degenerate (three wavefunctions with the same energy).
The subscripts $1$ and $2$ distinguish two different types of symmetry within the same degeneracy.
The state ${}^{4}A_{2}$ is the lowest in energy and is the ground state of Cr$^{3+}$ in corundum.
The states ${}^{4}T_{2}$ and ${}^{4}T_{1}$ lie higher in energy and correspond to two different excited configurations of the three-electron system.


\subsection{Optical Transitions and Absorption Spectrum}

\subsubsection{Overview of the Energy Levels}

The splitting of the Cr$^{3+}$ $d$-orbitals creates a set of energy levels that an electron can move between.
An overview of these states is given by the Tanabe-Sugano diagram for a $d^{3}$ ion (Fig.~\ref{fig:tanabe_sugano}).
The solid lines represent the quartet states, which originate from the free-ion ground term ${}^{4}F$, while the dashed lines represent doublet states that arise from higher-lying free-ion terms such as ${}^{2}G$.
Quartet states have total spin $S=\tfrac{3}{2}$ (superscript $4$), while doublet states have total spin $S=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (superscript $2$).
Transitions between states of the same spin are strong, whereas transitions between quartet and doublet states are much weaker.

\subsubsection{Broad Absorption Bands}

When a photon has exactly the right energy, one of the $d$-electrons can be excited from the ground state into one of the two excited quartet states.
The light at exactly this wavelength is removed from the transmitted beam, so the crystal absorbs certain parts of the spectrum more strongly than others.
Because the ${}^{4}T_{2}$ and ${}^{4}T_{1}$ states lie at different energies, the absorption spectrum shows two separate features rather than only one.

In a real crystal the surrounding ions are not perfectly fixed but vibrate, and the distances between the Cr$^{3+}$ ion and the ligands are slightly distorted.
These effects mean that the exact energy required for the excitation is not the same in every chromium site.
As a result the absorption does not occur at one sharp wavelength, but is spread out over a broader range, producing the wide absorption bands characteristic of ruby.

For Cr$^{3+}$ in corundum two such broad bands appear in the visible region.
The so-called Y band (${}^{4}A_{2} \rightarrow {}^{4}T_{2}$) and U band (${}^{4}A_{2} \rightarrow {}^{4}T_{1}$).
Their positions determine which parts of the spectrum are absorbed most strongly.
Since these two bands absorb most strongly in the green and blue part of the spectrum, red light is transmitted.
This is why ruby appears red in both transmission and reflected light.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{../resources/figures/TanabeSugano.png}
    \caption{Tanabe-Sugano diagram for a $d^{3}$ ion showing the dependence of the electronic term energies on the crystal-field strength $Dq/B$.
    At $Dq/B = 0$ the free-ion ground term ${}^{4}F$ appears as a single level, which splits into three states: the orange ${}^{4}A_{2}$ ground state, the blue ${}^{4}T_{2}$ state, and the green ${}^{4}T_{1}$ state~\cite{wikipedia_tanabe_sugano_d3}.}
    \label{fig:tanabe_sugano}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Forbidden Transitions}

In the deep red region the spectrum of ruby shows a few very weak and narrow features in addition to the broad U and Y bands.
These originate from transitions into doublet states such as ${}^{2}T_{1}$, ${}^{2}T_{2}$ and ${}^{2}E$.
Transitions that change the total spin are often called ``forbidden'', because they have a much lower probability than normal spin-conserving transitions.
However, real crystals are never perfectly symmetric, so small distortions and lattice vibrations mix the electronic states slightly and these transitions become weakly allowed.
As a result they appear as tiny absorption features in the red part of the spectrum.

The doublet state ${}^{2}E$ also plays an important role because electrons that are first excited into ${}^{4}T_{2}$ can relax non-radiatively into this level.
From there they can return to the ground state ${}^{4}A_{2}$ by emitting red light.
This radiative decay produces two very sharp emission lines around 694\,nm, often referred to as the R-lines.
They do not show up as absorption dips in this experiment, but they belong to the same group of spin-forbidden transitions and one of them forms the basis of the well-known ruby laser~\cite{kitzmann_molecular_ruby_2022,wikipedia_ruby_pressure_scale}.


\subsection{Determining the Crystal-Field Parameter \texorpdfstring{$Dq$}{}}

The Tanabe–Sugano diagram for a $d^{3}$ ion, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tanabe_sugano}, plots the energies of the electronic states as a function of the crystal-field strength.
In this diagram the term energies are expressed in spectroscopic units of $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, and the Racah parameter $B$ has the same units, so the vertical axis plots the dimensionless ratio $E/B$.
The crystal-field strength $Dq$ is likewise an energy expressed in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, which is why the horizontal axis shows the dimensionless ratio $Dq/B$.
Because both axes are dimensionless, measured transition energies can be placed on the diagram without any further scaling.

In ruby the ground state is ${}^{4}A_{2}$, and the two broad absorption bands in the visible range arise from excitations into the ${}^{4}T_{2}$ and ${}^{4}T_{1}$ states.
These are the Y band (${}^{4}A_{2}\rightarrow{}^{4}T_{2}$) and the U band (${}^{4}A_{2}\rightarrow{}^{4}T_{1}$).
To compare the measured wavelengths with the diagram, the photon energies are obtained from
\[
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda},
\]
or, in spectroscopic units,
\[
\tilde{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda}.
\]

Once the central energies (or wavenumbers) of the U and Y bands are known, they can be placed on the vertical axis of the Tanabe–Sugano diagram.
Each energy intersects its corresponding curve (either the ${}^{4}T_{2}$ or the ${}^{4}T_{1}$ line) and the intersection gives a value of $Dq/B$.
Since the two broad bands are wide and not perfectly sharp, the two values of $Dq/B$ are usually close but not identical, so their average provides the best estimate.
Using the Racah parameter $B = 765\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, the crystal-field parameter follows simply from
\[
Dq = B\cdot(Dq/B).
\]
After $Dq$ has been determined, it is sometimes convenient to express the strength of the octahedral field through the quantity
\[
\Delta = 10Dq.
\]
This relation comes from the simple one-electron picture, in which the five $d$-orbitals split into a lower $t_{2g}$ group and a higher $e_{g}$ group separated by $10Dq$.
For a multi-electron ion such as Cr$^{3+}$ the actual optical transitions involve many-electron states, so they do not occur exactly at $10Dq$, but the parameter $\Delta$ remains a traditional way of expressing the overall size of the crystal-field splitting.



% --- Experimental Setup ---
\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Overview}

To measure the transmission spectrum of the ruby sample, a two-beam absorption spectrometer is used.
The basic idea is that the lamp light is split into two beams, where one beam passes through the ruby (probe beam) and the other bypasses it (reference beam).
Because both beams originate from the same light source, any fluctuations of the lamp intensity affect them equally.
By comparing the two signals, these fluctuations are  suppressed and the true absorption of the sample is isolated.
A schematic of the setup is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:spectrometer}.


\subsection{Optical Layout and Beam Paths}

A xenon high-pressure lamp (XHL) produces broadband light across the visible range.
Immediately after the lamp, an iris diaphragm (IB1) limits the beam diameter and improves its spatial stability.
The beam then encounters a beam splitter (ST), which divides it into two separate light paths.
The upper path forms the probe beam (Strahlengang 1), while the lower path serves as the reference beam (Referenzstrahlengang).

Each path contains a lens (L) that focuses the light onto the element placed in that arm.
In the probe arm the light is focused onto a metal holder (Ru/LB) that contains the ruby and, directly below it, a fixed pinhole aperture (LB).
By raising or lowering this holder, the beam can be directed either through the ruby or through the pinhole.
In the reference arm the light is focused onto an identical fixed pinhole aperture (LB), but no ruby sample is present.

After the sample region both beams pass through additional iris diaphragms (IB2 in the reference arm and IB3 in the probe arm) and then through a second lens (L).
The beams are then recombined by another beam splitter (ST).
From this point onward they follow the same optical path toward the monochromator.

The monochromator (M) selects only a narrow wavelength band at a time.
Its diffraction grating is driven by a motor that scans continuously through a chosen wavelength interval within the visible spectrum.
The wavelength-selected beam finally reaches the photomultiplier tube (PM), whose signal is used for the measurement.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../resources/figures/spectrometer.png}
    \caption{Schematic of the two-beam absorption spectrometer used to record the transmission spectrum of the ruby crystal~\cite{ISOAnleitung}.}
    \label{fig:spectrometer}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Chopper and Lock-In Detection}

A key part of the setup is the mechanical chopper (C), which sits right before the sample region.
The chopper continuously alternates between blocking the probe beam and blocking the reference beam.
This means the detector receives an alternating sequence:
\[
\text{probe} \rightarrow \text{reference} \rightarrow \text{probe} \rightarrow \text{reference} \rightarrow \cdots
\]
The electrical signal from the photomultiplier is then sent to two places.
One branch goes directly to the oscilloscope (Oszi), where the alternating probe–reference pattern can be monitored during alignment and calibration.
The other branch goes into the A/D converter, where the signal is digitized and passed on to the lock-in amplifier.

The chopper controller provides a reference signal at the same modulation frequency, so the lock-in is synced precisely to the opening and closing of the two beam paths.
Using this timing information, the lock-in assigns a positive sign to the probe contribution and a negative sign to the reference contribution.
By averaging over many modulation cycles, the lock-in amplifier outputs a voltage proportional to the intensity difference
\[
I_{\mathrm{Probe}} - I_{\mathrm{Reference}}.
\]
This technique is highly effective at removing noise.
Slow drifts of the lamp, ambient light, or electrical disturbances affect both beams equally and are therefore canceled out.
What remains is the small difference caused by the ruby absorbing certain wavelengths of the probe beam.

\subsection{Data Acquisition}

The output of the lock-in amplifier is recorded by the computer as the monochromator scans the selected wavelength interval.
The resulting dataset contains the wavelength-dependent difference signal, from which the transmission spectrum of the ruby sample is obtained.
This spectrum includes both the broad U and Y absorption bands and the much weaker spin-forbidden transitions in the deep red region.



% --- Procedure ---
\section{Procedure}

The measurements followed the sequence of tasks described in the experiment manual.
All measurements were carried out by manually starting and stopping both the monochromator motor and the LabVIEW data acquisition software at the same time.
This introduces a small reaction-time offset at the beginning and end of each scan.
For each scan the initial value (\textit{Startwert}) in nanometers, the gear factor (\textit{Getriebefaktor}), and the scan direction (\textit{Getrieberichtung}) were entered into the LabVIEW interface~\cite{ISO_Datenaufnahme}.

At the beginning of each data file these three values are stored as metadata, followed by two columns containing the time in seconds and the lock-in output voltage in volts.
The LabVIEW front panel labels this quantity as ``Amplitude [mV]'' or equivalently as ``Intensität'', but the numerical values saved in the data files are written in SI units (volts).

\subsection{Initial Alignment of the Spectrometer}

Before the main measurements were recorded the two beams were aligned so that both of them were centered on the entrance slit of the monochromator.
The wavelength was then set to 700\,nm, where the ruby absorbs negligibly.
At this wavelength the intensities of the probe and reference beams were balanced using the diaphragms IB1 and IB2, with IB3 kept fully open.
The balancing was monitored on the oscilloscope, which displayed the lock-in output as an alternating two-level signal corresponding to probe and reference.
If the two plateaus did not lie at the same vertical height the diaphragms were adjusted until both levels matched.
This ensured that both arms delivered equal intensity to the detector before the actual spectra were recorded.

\subsection{Difference Measurement of Probe and Reference Beams}

The first full measurement recorded the difference between probe and reference signals.
The wavelength was scanned from 350\,nm to 720\,nm using a lock-in sensitivity of 50\,mV.
A gear factor of 3.6 was used, corresponding to a scan speed of 1\,nm/s.
The scan direction was noted in the data file, where a value of $+1$ indicates a scan from short to long wavelengths and $-1$ indicates the reverse direction.
This measurement provided the coarse difference spectrum of the sample against the reference beam.

\subsection{Intensity Spectrum of the Xenon Lamp}

To record the intensity distribution of the xenon lamp the probe beam was blocked using aluminum foil placed directly in its path.
The chopper and lock-in amplifier remained in the same configuration as before.
With only the reference beam reaching the detector a full 350–720\,nm scan was recorded at 50\,mV sensitivity and with the same gear factor of 3.6 (1\,nm/s).
This produced the reference-beam-only intensity spectrum of the light source.

\subsection{Intensity Spectrum of the Probe Beam}

The intensity spectrum of the probe beam alone was obtained by blocking the reference arm in the same way as above.
With the probe beam unblocked and the reference beam blocked, the system recorded a second single-beam spectrum over 350–720\,nm at 50\,mV sensitivity using the same gear factor of 3.6 (1\,nm/s).

\subsection{High-Sensitivity Measurements of the Red Region}

To observe the weak spin-forbidden transitions in the deep red region the sensitivity of the lock-in amplifier was increased to 5\,mV.
In this case the gear factor was reduced to 2.16, corresponding to a slower scan speed of 0.6\,nm/s.
The monochromator was scanned over the restricted range 670-720\,nm.
Three measurements were recorded in this region.
First the difference spectrum was recorded with both beams unblocked.
Second a reference-beam-only spectrum was obtained by blocking the probe beam.
Third a probe-beam-only spectrum was recorded by blocking the reference beam.

\subsection{Baseline Measurement with Pinhole}

To determine the baseline of the apparatus the ruby crystal was replaced by the pinhole aperture in the probe arm.
At 700\,nm the intensities of the two beams were matched again by adjusting IB1, IB2 and IB3.
A full baseline spectrum was recorded from 350–720\,nm at 50\,mV sensitivity using a gear factor of 3.6 (1\,nm/s).
In addition, a high-sensitivity baseline was recorded over 670–720\,nm at 5\,mV with the slower gear factor of 2.16 (0.6\,nm/s), to verify that any tiny features in the red region originated from the ruby sample and not from the setup.

\subsection{Wavelength Calibration Using a Mercury Lamp}

For wavelength calibration a mercury low-pressure lamp was placed directly in front of the entrance slit of the monochromator.
In this configuration the chopper and lock-in amplifier were switched off, since the calibration spectrum is recorded as a direct intensity signal.
A scan over the full range 350–720\,nm was recorded using the standard coarse-scan gear factor of 3.6 (1\,nm/s).
The sharp emission lines of mercury were later used to calibrate the wavelength scale of all other spectra.



% --- Results ---
\section{Results}
\subsection{Wavelength Calibration of the Monochromator}

To calibrate the wavelength scale of the monochromator we recorded the emission spectrum of a mercury low-pressure lamp and compared the measured peak positions to the known reference wavelengths.
The recorded spectrum and the literature spectrum are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hg_measured} and Figure~\ref{fig:hg_reference}.
Because the monochromator scans mechanically and the motor does not have an absolute zero point, small wavelength offsets of a few nanometers are common and must be corrected.

The measured mercury spectrum shows several sharp lines on top of a very low background.
One extremely strong peak appears near 512\,nm.
This does not correspond to any real mercury line in the visible range.
It is a first-order diffraction peak of the ultraviolet 253.7\,nm line and is therefore ignored in the calibration.

All other peaks in the recorded spectrum match the characteristic mercury lines and can be used for the calibration.
By inspecting the plotted spectrum we first estimated the approximate positions of the visible peaks by eye.
For each of these guesses we then searched the data in a small interval of $\pm 3$\,nm and selected the exact wavelength at which the measured intensity reached its maximum.

The following reference wavelengths were used:
\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}} = (365.0,\; 404.7,\; 435.8,\; 546.1,\; 577.0,\; 579.0)\,\mathrm{nm}.
\]
The corresponding measured peak positions were found to be:
\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{meas}} = (369.3,\; 409.0,\; 440.4,\; 550.8,\; 581.7,\; 583.8)\,\mathrm{nm}.
\]

For each line we formed the wavelength difference
\[
\Delta\lambda_i = \lambda_{\mathrm{ref},i} - \lambda_{\mathrm{meas},i}.
\]
All six values were close to one another, with a mean shift of
\[
\Delta\lambda = -4.57\,\mathrm{nm}.
\]

This constant offset was applied to all other spectra in the form
\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{corr}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{meas}} + \Delta\lambda.
\]
After this correction the mercury lines align well with the literature values and the wavelength scale of the monochromator is considered calibrated.
